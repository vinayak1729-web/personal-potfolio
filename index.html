Real-time Greeks & IV Analytics Dashboard

Live Delta, Gamma, Theta and Vega across every leg of a strategy, alongside synthetic-ATM option chains and IV skew heatmaps — built so a trader sees not just P&L, but why it moved.

Show breakdown
Why this exists: a trader who only watches price is blind to why the P&L moved. Watching all four Greeks together tells you whether today's move came from direction, acceleration of that direction, time decay, or a shift in the market's fear — and that decides whether to hold, hedge, or exit.
Δ
Delta

How much an option's price moves for a ₹1 move in the underlying — effectively your directional exposure. Traders watch net Delta to know how long or short the book really is right now, not just on paper.

Γ
Gamma

How fast Delta itself changes as the underlying moves — the risk that a "safe" position suddenly isn't. High Gamma near expiry is why 0-DTE options can swing a book violently in minutes.

Θ
Theta

The daily cost — or income, if you're a seller — of time passing. It's the rent every option position pays or collects; premium-selling strategies live and die by Theta versus the other Greeks.

ν
Vega

Sensitivity to implied volatility itself, independent of direction. A position can lose money on a directionally correct call if IV crushes hard enough after the move.

Building the order book — market-data engine (C++)

One raw multicast packet → one accurate, ranked order book, tens of thousands of times a second

Show technical breakdown

Every listed strike needs its own live limit order book, rebuilt tick by tick, with no room for a slow allocation or a blocking lock anywhere near the hot path. Here's the pipeline, piece by piece, and why each one is built the way it is:

  • Lock-free SPSC ring buffer: incoming multicast packets land in a single-producer/single-consumer ring buffer sized as a power of two, so wraparound is a fast bitmask instead of a modulo operation, and the read/write pointers are padded onto their own cache lines. This lets the network-receive thread and the book-building thread run flat out on separate CPU cores without ever taking a lock or waiting on each other — the classic bottleneck in any tick-processing pipeline.
  • CPU core pinning: every hot thread — the multicast receiver, the book builder, the snapshot sender — is pinned to its own core via thread affinity, so the OS scheduler never migrates it mid-burst and its working data stays warm in that core's cache. That's the difference between consistently low microsecond latency and occasional multi-millisecond stalls that show up as "random" slippage.
  • Free-list pool allocator for orders: every individual resting order lives in a preallocated array — nothing is allocated on the hot path. A stack of free slot indices hands out and reclaims order slots in O(1), so tens of thousands of adds, modifies and cancels a second never touch a memory allocator or fragment memory over the course of a session.
  • Hash-map order index: a hash map keyed on the exchange's own order ID resolves straight to that order's slot in the pool in O(1). This matters because a modify or cancel message references an order ID, not a price — the book has to find and mutate the exact right order instantly, out of tens of thousands resting at any moment.
  • Direct-indexed price-level array — the book itself: rather than a sorted tree or map of price levels (an O(log n) update every single time), price levels live in one large flat array indexed directly by price-tick, so updating a level is a single array write — O(1), not O(log n). Best-bid and best-ask pointers only ever step forward from wherever they last were, so re-finding the top of book after a change is also O(1) amortized, never a full re-scan of the ladder.
  • Packed binary snapshot format: the book is exported as a fixed-layout, byte-packed struct — no padding, no JSON — so a full 20-level, both-sides snapshot with timestamp and trade info for one instrument fits in a few hundred bytes. That compact wire format is what makes storing a full day of tick history ~10–15× smaller than the equivalent JSON.
  • Sequence-gap detection & recovery: every multicast stream is sequence-numbered; the engine tracks the next expected sequence number per stream, and any gap, duplicate or reset is counted and reconciled against exchange snapshots rather than silently ignored. A dropped packet becomes a detected, repaired event instead of an invisible source of a wrong book.
  • Zero-copy, zero-tick-loss forwarding: downstream, a second ring-buffered service receives those binary snapshots over a Unix domain socket and fans them out to every consumer — loggers, live strategy signal-builders — at once. Busy-wait polling between checks uses a CPU pause instruction rather than sleeping, trading a small amount of CPU time for the lowest possible latency instead of scheduler jitter from waking a sleeping thread.

Execution & risk layer — Smart-Netting OMS/RMS

Everything between "the model says trade" and "the broker has the position"

Show technical breakdown

A signal is only as good as the order it actually turns into. This layer sits between every strategy's decision and the broker, and is where cost, compliance and crash-safety are actually enforced:

  • Signal aggregation / netting: several sub-strategies can independently want a position in the same instrument at the same instant. Before anything reaches the broker, every sub-strategy's target is summed into one net target per instrument, and only the delta between that and the current live position is what actually gets ordered — eliminating fully-offsetting trades that would otherwise cost brokerage and slippage for zero net change in exposure.
  • ZeroMQ pub/sub messaging: strategy signals are published over ZeroMQ rather than polled over HTTP, so the netting layer reacts the instant a signal is published instead of on the next poll cycle — removing an entire class of latency that comes down to simply "how often did we bother to ask."
  • Order slicing for exchange compliance: exchanges cap the quantity permitted per order; the engine automatically slices a large net order into compliant chunks and paces them out, instead of requiring every individual strategy to know and respect that limit itself.
  • Per-strategy risk guardrails: position limits are enforced per strategy — some constrained long-only — independent of the netting math, so one mis-behaving signal can never silently override another strategy's risk budget.
  • Database as single source of truth: every position and order is written synchronously to a relational database, not just held in memory. If a process crashes or the server reboots mid-session, it queries its own last known state on restart and rebuilds its exact market exposure instead of guessing — or worse, doubling a position it forgot it already held.
  • Emergency square-off kill switch: a separate, deliberately minimal tool that bypasses all strategy logic entirely and can flatten every open position by querying the database directly. It's the one button that has to work even if everything else on the desk is on fire.

04 Research How frequency, not just direction, decides which edge fits which market

Same underlying discipline — regime detection, adaptive risk — applied wherever the signal actually lives: from the shape of implied volatility across a session, down to the shape of the order book in microseconds. Described here in market terms, not in the underlying code. Open the Research Journey tab on the right edge to see how it evolved, then the strategies by frequency below.

Medium-Frequency Strategies

Holding period: minutes to full session

Momentum energy models

An econophysics-style read on trend — price momentum treated like kinetic energy, and its distance from a fair-value anchor (VWAP/EMA) treated like potential energy, with exits triggered on the first signs of deceleration rather than a lagging crossover.

Market behavior

Performs best in genuinely trending sessions where a move keeps adding energy in the same direction, and gives back less on the way down by exiting as momentum fades instead of waiting for confirmation. Struggles in choppy, directionless sessions where price oscillates without ever building sustained momentum.

Go deeper

Show more

The exit condition specifically watches for deceleration — the rate at which momentum itself is slowing — rather than waiting for a moving-average crossover, which by construction lags the actual turn. In practice this can mean exiting a trade several bars before a classic crossover-based system would even register the reversal, at the cost of occasionally exiting into what turns out to be only a brief pause rather than a genuine top.

This family grew out of a physics analogy — price as a system exchanging potential energy (distance from equilibrium) for kinetic energy (velocity) — applied to range-bound days. It was tested on March 2025 and did poorly, because that window was a one-directional panic-selling shock rather than a range. The lesson kept: separate “the model failed” from “the regime wasn't the one it was built for.”

VWAP-anchored trend signal

Watches price velocity relative to VWAP, paired with a stop-loss that tightens automatically as days-to-expiry shrinks — because Theta and Gamma risk both rise sharply as expiry nears.

Market behavior

Suited to sessions building a clear directional bias through the day; risk naturally shrinks on expiry day and loosens further from expiry. Underperforms on quiet, low-realized-volatility days where price never moves far enough from VWAP to trigger a real signal.

Go deeper

Show more

The adaptive stop doesn't move by a fixed number of points — it scales with realized short-term volatility and is compressed further by how many days remain to expiry, so the same signal carries a materially different stop distance on a five-day-to-expiry session versus expiry day itself. That keeps risk roughly comparable in rupee terms even as Gamma and Theta both accelerate sharply into the final sessions.

Premium-fraction options structuring

Non-directional, Theta-harvesting structures — Iron Condor, Broken-Wing Butterfly, ratio spreads — where strikes are chosen by what fraction of the ATM premium they cost, not a fixed strike distance or fixed Delta.

Market behavior

Normalizes risk across calm and volatile days: on high-IV days the structure automatically widens, on low-IV days it tightens, keeping exposure comparable session to session. Profits from range-bound, Theta-friendly days; most exposed to sudden gaps or a sharp volatility expansion a static structure can't react to intraday.

Go deeper

Show more

Because strikes are chosen by premium fraction rather than fixed distance or fixed Delta, the same instruction — say, sell the option at roughly three-quarters of the ATM premium and buy the one at roughly a quarter — produces a wide structure on a high-VIX day and a tight one on a low-VIX day automatically, with no manual re-parameterization needed as the volatility regime shifts session to session.

Rolling walk-forward validation

Every medium-frequency idea above is tested across rolling multi-year tranches rather than one long backtest window, with realistic slippage, transaction costs, and Indian tax drag applied throughout.

Market behavior

Surfaces whether a strategy actually survives distinct macro regimes — a sharp crash, a grinding low-volatility year — instead of looking good only because one favorable period dominates the sample.

Go deeper

Show more

Beyond the headline return, every tranche is also scored on higher-order moments — skew and kurtosis of the return distribution — because a strategy with excellent average returns but strongly negative skew is quietly building toward one large loss, which a Sharpe ratio alone won't reveal.

Velocity & Runner — intraday trend

Two intraday trend strategies that enter on the momentum strength of the option or asset being traded. Velocity takes profit at a target scaled to the index's ATM implied volatility; Runner drops the fixed target and the forced end-of-day exit to ride the whole move.

Market behavior

Velocity captures ordinary directional days cleanly, and its target breathes with real-time volatility instead of sitting at fixed points. Runner earns the most on strong momentum days that run past the normal band. Both lose on choppy days where the index oscillates inside its usual deviation band — which is exactly the gap the Energy research was started to close.

Go deeper

Show more

The stop is DTE-aware: it tightens as expiry approaches and loosens further out, because the same move means very different things to an option with six days left and one with hours left — theta and gamma risk are not constant across the cycle.

Runner's trade-off is deliberate: it gives up the certainty of a target to avoid missing the day's biggest move, so its P&L is lumpier but its winners are larger.

Swing variant — OBI + drift, longer horizon

Derived from the same order-book-imbalance and drift framework as the high-frequency work, but held over a longer horizon — letting a confirmed trend run instead of scalping it.

Market behavior

Fewer trades, so cost drag per unit of edge falls, and it holds through intraday noise once drift confirms direction. It is weakest when drift flips quickly or an overnight gap lands against the position. This variant has also worked in practice.

Show more

Because it reuses the proven signal framework rather than inventing a new one, the swing version inherits the same causal, no-look-ahead discipline as the faster strategies.

High-Frequency Strategies

Holding period: seconds to a few minutes

Order-book imbalance & liquidity dominance

Measures the volume of large resting orders on the bid versus the ask across several rolling lookback windows, to read which side of the book genuine size is stacking on.

Market behavior

Markets tend to move toward where liquidity is being pulled or stacked, so this reads directional pressure before it shows up in price. Cross-checking resting size that persists for several ticks against one-tick flickers helps separate real institutional interest from spoofing; most reliable in liquid, actively-traded strikes and loses edge in thin, wide-spread contracts.

Go deeper

Show more

Resting size is read across several rolling lookback windows at once — short, medium and longer horizons — rather than a single window, so the signal distinguishes a genuinely building imbalance from a brief, one-tick flicker of size that vanishes as fast as it appeared. Depth sitting right at the top of book is weighted more heavily than depth resting further away, mirroring how a trader reading the tape would naturally read it.

Order-flow confirmation on the tape

A short-horizon signal built from actual traded buy/sell flow — not resting orders — requiring flow and price to agree on direction for a couple of consecutive bars before it commits.

Market behavior

Keeps the strategy out of moves where price drifts but nobody's actually pressing the trade — the kind of move that reverses just as fast. Performs well in liquid, directionally-trending tape; gets whipsawed in low-volume, indecisive periods where flow flips faster than price can confirm it.

Go deeper

Show more

This is deliberately a two-bar-confirmation signal rather than a one-tick trigger: the first bar establishes that flow and price agree, and the second confirms it wasn't a single print. That one extra bar of patience is a direct trade-off between reacting faster and reacting to something that turns out to be noise — tuned firmly toward the latter.

Microsecond execution layer

The lock-free, core-pinned C++ path that acts on these signals — infrastructure, not a strategy in itself, but the layer that decides whether a genuine signal is actually captured.

Market behavior

In HFT, being a few hundred microseconds slower than the rest of the market is functionally the same as being wrong, even when the signal was right. This layer determines how much of a genuine edge is collected versus lost to latency.

Go deeper

Show more

Because the strategy layer and the execution layer are architecturally separate, the execution path can be re-optimized — faster parsing, a leaner network transport — without touching a single line of the logic that decides direction, and vice versa. That separation is itself a risk decision: it keeps a latency experiment from being able to accidentally change what a strategy believes about the market.

Premium & liquidity gatekeeping

A guardrail that refuses entries when option premium falls below a minimum threshold, regardless of how strong the underlying signal looks.

Market behavior

Protects against Gamma risk and liquidity traps in deep out-of-the-money options near expiry, where the bid-ask spread can be wider than the theoretical edge the signal is chasing — a filter that costs a few trades to avoid the ones that would have lost regardless of direction.

Go deeper

Show more

The threshold isn't about the strategy's view being wrong — it's about the cost of trading it. Below a certain premium, the bid-ask spread on a deep out-of-the-money option can be a large fraction of the option's entire value, meaning the position would need an outsized favorable move just to cover the cost of entering and exiting, before any edge from the signal itself is realized.

Volume Run — order-flow engine

The production order-flow strategy. It measures buy versus sell pressure from the order book across several lookback windows at once, compares a fast read of that flow against a slow one, and requires price to confirm before it commits — trading a small, expiry-adaptive set of ATM and near-OTM options. Position state moves between flat, long and short, with confirmation over consecutive bars on both entry and exit.

Market behavior

Strongest when flow and price push the same way — directional bursts and post-event moves. In flat, low-volume tape it can be whipsawed, which is why the confirmation requirement exists. And because it holds several strikes at once, it carries residual exposure that isn't visible from any single position — the problem the dynamic hedge below is built around.

Go deeper

Show more

Multiple windows let it tell a building imbalance from a one-tick flicker. Requiring price confirmation keeps it out of moves where size is resting but nobody is actually pushing the market.

Net exposure is not just “add up the quantities”: calls and puts, near-the-money and far strikes, and long and short legs all respond very differently to the same move.

Dynamic scalping hedge for Volume Run

A hedge that reacts to what the strategy actually holds, instead of carrying the same protective structure all day. Today a predefined multi-leg options structure (Static V3) is held alongside Volume Run as partial protection — but it is static: it never looks at Volume Run's live book. The research goal is a hedge engine that reads the live exposure — which strikes, calls or puts, long or short, how large — aggregates the delta-, gamma- and vega-style sensitivity of the whole position, decides whether a hedge is warranted at all, and then takes and removes a temporary hedge.

Market behavior

Designed as a hybrid: a slower, minute-level read of state authorizes a hedge, and tick-level timing handles the entry and exit — so the decision doesn't flip on every tick, but execution is still responsive. The risk it has to manage is its own cost: hedging too eagerly just converts protection into spread and churn, so “no hedge” is a first-class outcome.

Go deeper

Show more

It is evaluated against four setups on equal footing — Volume Run alone; with the static hedge; with the dynamic hedge; and with both — so the dynamic version has to earn its place on combined P&L and drawdown, not on a flattering chart.

The first step is mathematical, not code: define precisely what exposure Volume Run carries and what a hedge should neutralize, before picking any indicator. It is built beside the existing live path, so the working strategy stays untouched.

How the research evolved

Each strategy fixes the failure of the one before it

Math and physics first, then markets. Every step below started from a specific failure — a stop that didn't survive expiry, a trend system that bled in chop, a volume rule that decayed — and the next strategy was designed to answer exactly that. The engineering that made each step possible is noted in between.

01Velocity

Intraday trend strategy with volatility-scaled targets

Show details
What it does

Originally briefed around protecting an intraday “box” structure. Entries trigger on the force — the momentum strength — of the option or asset being traded, and targets come from the index's ATM implied volatility, so they stretch and shrink with real-time volatility instead of sitting at fixed points.

What broke

The stop-loss. A fixed stop doesn't work on options because behavior changes sharply with days to expiry — a DTE-6 option and a DTE-0/1 option react completely differently to the same move.

Under the hood

Runs on the live state built from the C++ order-book feed; the ATM-IV target comes from the data gateway's synthetic-ATM calculation, so volatility is read in real time rather than from a stale table.

02DTE-aware stop-loss

A stop that adapts to theta risk

Show details
What it does

Tightens as expiry approaches and loosens further out, so the stop width matches how violently an option can move for the time it has left.

What broke

Velocity still exited at its IV-based target — and so missed any move that carried beyond the volatility band.

Under the hood

Days-to-expiry is derived from contract metadata at runtime, so the same rule applies consistently across every strike and expiry in the universe.

03Runner

No fixed target, no forced end-of-day exit

Show details
What it does

Rides the full move on strong momentum days instead of capping it at the volatility band.

What broke

Choppy, sideways days — where the index stays inside its normal deviation band — are loss-making for both Velocity and Runner. A trend strategy can't fix a day that has no trend.

Under the hood

Because a position can outlive a session, position state is kept in the database as the source of truth, so a restart recovers the exact exposure instead of guessing.

04Energy

Potential-to-kinetic model for range-bound days

Show details
What it does

The choppy-day gap was treated as its own research problem rather than patched into the trend strategies. Inspired by Walter Lewin's lectures on potential and kinetic energy, range-bound price action is modeled as potential energy (distance from equilibrium) converting into kinetic energy (price velocity) — to find turning points and exhaustion inside a range.

What broke

Tested on March 2025, it performed poorly. That window was a market-wide panic-selling regime — a one-directional liquidity shock, not the range-bound chop the model was built for.

The lesson

Separate “the model failed” from “the regime wasn't the one it was designed for.” It's the reason regime detection became a research track of its own.

Under the hood

Evaluated in the parallelized walk-forward backtest suite with realistic costs, so a bad window shows up as a bad window — not as a hidden average.

05HFT pivot

From bar-level strategies to microstructure

Show details
What it does

Moved to microstructure strategies on options and futures. First a simple bid-ask spread strategy, then a volume-threshold futures strategy: if traded volume crossed a fixed threshold, trade the side that drove it.

What broke

It worked at first, then started losing money. Rising volume on one side doesn't reliably predict the next price move — the assumption behind it wasn't robust, so the edge decayed.

Under the hood

Tick-level work only became possible because of the C++ order-book engine: full-depth snapshots streamed over Unix sockets and ring buffers with no tick loss.

06OBI — Order Book Imbalance

Replace “is volume rising” with “is price actually being pushed”

Show details
What it does

The arbitrary static threshold is gone. Bid-side and ask-side volume are collected into window-based baskets and turned into an imbalance ratio, which is then amplified when the change in traded price confirms it — flow only counts when price agrees.

What broke

A strong signal, but small moves in last-traded price could swing the ratio between its extremes and trigger excessive trades.

Under the hood

The baskets are built in the C++ signal builder over several rolling windows, weighted toward the top of the book, and updated in constant time per tick.

07Drift filter + swing variant

Let a trend through only once momentum is established

Show details
What it does

A drift/momentum filter suppresses small chops and releases a signal only once momentum is established — cutting overtrading while keeping the directional edge. From the same OBI + drift framework, a swing-trading version with a longer holding horizon was also derived, and it has worked too.

Under the hood

The filter is strictly causal — it only ever uses data available at that moment — so what shows up in a backtest is what could have happened live.

09Dynamic scalping hedge

Hedging what the strategy actually holds

Show details
What it does

Replacing an all-day static protective structure with a hedge that reads the live exposure of the order-flow strategy and acts only when it's needed. Full write-up in the High-Frequency section of Research.
